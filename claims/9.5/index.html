9.5 Stevens’s law, not only Weber’s law, prices exaggeration

Claim

Sensory ecology adopted Weber’s law of discriminability without Stevens’s power law of magnitude, and proportional processing alone does not limit ornament exaggeration: the limit inferred since Cohen (1984) comes from pricing cost in absolute units and benefit in proportional units.

An audit of nine papers from 1984 to 2026 found 246 uses of Weber’s law and no use of Stevens’s power law outside reference lists. Weber’s law is scale-free, so proportional discrimination sets no size limit by itself. With a perceived-area exponent near 0.76 (human data), looking twice as large costs about 2.5 times the area. The cost of apparent size depends on how the receiver’s magnitude scale maps projected dimensions.

What is new

Stevens (1957) and Teghtsoonian (1965) give magnitude exponents in humans; Cohen (1984) and Akre & Johnsen (2014) apply Weber’s law to signal evolution. New: bringing the magnitude scale into signal-evolution theory and locating the exaggeration limit in the cost currency, not in proportional processing.

Prior art

Stevens 1957; Stevens & Guirao 1963; Teghtsoonian 1965; Baird 1970; Cohen 1984; Akre & Johnsen 2014.

Test and evidence

Literature audit and derivation in a companion analysis of perceived magnitude in signal evolution. 2^(1/0.76) = 2.49.

Falsifier

A bisection or magnitude-estimation assay in a signalling receiver that gives an area exponent equal to the length exponent; or a model in which proportional processing alone, with cost and benefit in the same units, bounds exaggeration.

An outcome that contradicts the claim as stated.
