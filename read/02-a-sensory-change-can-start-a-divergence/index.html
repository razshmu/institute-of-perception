A sensory change can start a divergence

The question

Two populations of the same species come to differ in what they eat, where they live and whom they mate with. The standard account runs from outside in: the environment differed first, selection followed, and the animals’ senses were tuned to the conditions they found themselves in. Perception appears at the end of that story, as something adjusted.

The question this thesis asks is whether perception can appear at the beginning instead. Not as a metaphor, but in a form specific enough to fail: can a heritable change in a receiver start a divergence while the cue field stays the same?

What the thesis says

The Natural Perception Hypothesis places the target of selection in perceptual processing — the weighting, integration and stabilisation of sensory signals — and not only in receptors. That location matters. A receptor change adds or removes access to a band of signals. A processing change alters which differences an organism can act on, and it does so across every context at once.

This is the step that makes the rest follow. A single heritable change in processing does not improve the detection of one stimulus class and leave the rest of the animal’s world intact. It reorganises what stands out and what recedes in foraging, in habitat choice, in predator avoidance and in mate choice together.

That much is the received position rather than a new one. Sensory drive has held since Endler that receivers are shared across foraging, predation and mate choice, and that selection on one carries into the others. Fuller, Houle and Travis proposed testing the resulting correlated response by reciprocal selection twenty years ago. What is at issue here is the breadth of the carry-over and where the target of selection sits, and both are quantities to be measured rather than premises. The falsifier is pitched at that level. Take a heritable perceptual variant that changes discrimination of its target signal class only, with no consequence for foraging, habitat use or mate choice under matched cue fields. That refutes it.

From here the receiver-first claim is a short step. If one perceptual change touches many behaviours, then a change in receiver sensory state — an opsin variant, an odorant-receptor variant — can start divergence in mate choice and habitat preference while the focal cue field is held constant. The environment need not move first.

Two guards are built into the thesis rather than added afterwards. The first is that it is a claim about frequency, not about necessity. How often receiver-first routes operate is treated as an empirical question, settled system by system, with stated conditions under which a receiver-first reading is abandoned. The second is a scope condition: receiver-first routes are worth testing where three things coincide — receiver differences are repeatable or heritable, they redirect a behaviour with ecological reach, and that behaviour changes the cue, resource or reproductive field. Outside those conditions the thesis does not apply, and says so.

A formal reason favours amplification over a small local effect. If one sensory feature enters the perception of many environmental traits, a small change in that feature shifts all of them together, and the selection gradient on it scales with the number of traits it touches. A receiver parameter is not one trait among many. It is a term that appears in many places at once.

The sharp form of this is a claim about counting, and Chapter 11 states it: if a few calibration parameters mediate many tasks, the genetic covariance measured across those tasks should have fewer effective dimensions than there are tasks.

A worked example

Primate trichromacy is a candidate receiver-first loop, and it is instructive because it is a candidate rather than a demonstration.

The proposed sequence is this. A change in the L/M opsins improved red-green discrimination. Improved discrimination shifted fruit choice toward red-shifted fruit. Shifted fruit choice altered which seeds were carried and deposited where. That, in turn, changed selection on fruit colour and — through the returning cue field — on colour vision itself. The loop closes: receiver state to behaviour, behaviour to environment, environment back to receiver.

What makes this a usable example rather than a story is that what would break it can be said exactly. Within polymorphic platyrrhine groups, where trichromats and dichromats occur in the same populations, the two should differ in their choice of red-shifted fruit and in where they deposit seeds. If they do not, or if fruit colour shows no association with the visual systems of its dispersers, the loop is not running in that system.

The same structure has a practical use. Where turbidity, artificial light or noise collapses the sensory discrimination that keeps two populations apart, restoring sensory contrast is a conservation intervention aimed at the receiver rather than at habitat area. That prediction is falsifiable in the ordinary way: if restoring contrast in a masked system does not restore assortative mating, or if managing habitat area does as well or better, the claim fails there.

What would refute it

The thesis is refutable at three levels, each stated separately.

At the level of the core claim: a heritable perceptual variant whose effect is confined to its own signal class, leaving foraging, habitat use and mate choice unchanged under matched cue fields.

At the level of the receiver-first route: lines differing only in an edited receiver gene that show no divergence in mate choice or habitat preference under matched cue fields. Divergence appearing only after the cue field has itself changed does the same damage, since it makes the environment first after all.

At the level of the loop: a system in which the cue field does not diverge when feedback is allowed, or in which it diverges just as much when masking removes the behavioural differences the receiver was supposed to cause.

The direct test is specified rather than gestured at. Edit one opsin, phototransduction or odorant-receptor gene in a fly or a fish, hold the focal cue field inside a declared tolerance band, and measure mate choice, habitat preference and environmental modification against unedited siblings. The design is demanding but ordinary; nothing in it requires a new technique.

Two terms introduced here

The thesis contributes two pieces of vocabulary that the later chapters use.

A perceptual niche is the set of cue differences a given organism can register and act on. One habitat, measured in the usual physical terms, supports more than one perceptual niche at once, and a sensory variant defines a further one without any change in the measured habitat. This is what allows divergence without environmental divergence: the animals have moved apart in a space the habitat measurement does not index.

Perceptual decline and ascent name the two directions of change in that space — the narrowing or coarsening of a range of differences formerly available to a lineage, and the appearance or sharpening of a discrimination. Both are defined relative to a stated baseline, which is what keeps them from being evaluative terms.

The claims this chapter rests on

  • 1.1
    Natural Perception Hypothesis (NPH) Central thesis

    Natural selection acts on perceptual processing (the weighting, integration and stabilisation of sensory signals), and a heritable change in that processing changes which differences an organism can act on across contexts.

  • 1.2
    Receiver-first evolution (RFE) Central thesis

    A heritable change in receiver sensory state, such as an opsin or odorant-receptor variant, starts divergence in mate choice and habitat preference while the focal cue field is held constant.

  • 1.5
    Receiver-side pleiotropy: sensory-biased magic traits Specific extension

    One change in receiver perception couples ecological performance and mate choice, so the pleiotropy of a sensory-biased magic trait resides in the receiver rather than in the sender’s trait genes.

  • 1.6
    Primate trichromacy as candidate receiver-first loop Testable hypothesis

    Primate trichromacy is a candidate receiver-first loop: an L/M opsin change improved red-green discrimination, shifted fruit choice, altered seed dispersal, and so changed selection on fruit colour and on colour vision.

  • 1.13
    Receiver-first niche construction motif Terminological proposal

    Receiver-first niche construction is the causal motif Os → Oi → E → (Os, Oi). Heritable receiver-state differences bias behaviour, the behaviour durably modifies the cue or resource field, and the modified field returns to affect later…

  • 1.14
    Perceptual niche Terminological proposal

    A perceptual niche is the set of cue differences a given organism can register and act on; one measured habitat supports several perceptual niches, and a sensory variant defines a new one without any change in the measured habitat.
