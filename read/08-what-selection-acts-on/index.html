What selection acts on

The question

The preceding chapters have been dismantling a boundary. The environment is not a neutral backdrop, because which differences it contains depends on the receiver. The trait is not a property of the organism alone, because what it does depends on who receives it. The body’s own partition is an achievement rather than a given.

If all of that holds, a question falls due. Selection acts on something. What, exactly, is the thing?

What the thesis says

This thesis is one claim, and it is short because it is a consequence rather than a proposal. Within the scientific description, the interactor is the organism coupled to its perceived and enacted field. The replicator is the heritable receiver architecture.

The vocabulary is borrowed deliberately. In the standard account, the interactor is the entity that interacts with its environment in a way that makes replication differential, and the replicator is what passes on. The usual answers are the organism and the gene. The structure stays and both terms change.

The interactor becomes the coupling, not the organism, because the previous four chapters removed the alternative. If what a trait does depends on the receiver, if the environment’s differences are receiver-relative, and if the body’s boundary is drawn by the same architecture, then the organism alone is not the unit that interacts. The coupling is.

The replicator becomes the receiver architecture — not the realised organisation, which depends on the environment too, but the parameters the organism carries and transmits. This is the same distinction Chapter 6 drew between architecture and realised organisation, now stated at the level of inheritance.

Both halves are qualified by the phrase “within the scientific description”. This is not a discovery about what selection really acts on, in a metaphysical sense. It is a choice of unit for a description, and choices of unit are the kind of thing that can be evaluated by whether they predict.

A worked example

The clearest illustration is the one from Chapter 6, read in reverse.

The same appendage, the same physical variation, two receiver mappings, two different selection gradients. If the organism were the interactor, this could not happen: the same organism with the same appendage in the same physical environment would face the same selection. It happens because the thing under selection includes the mapping.

The example also shows why the replicator has to be the architecture rather than the realised organisation. The realised organisation differed between the two cases, but nothing in the organism’s inheritance differed — the mapping was the variable. What an organism transmits is the capacity to register and group in a certain way; what results from that capacity depends on what it meets.

What would refute it

This claim carries no falsifier of its own, for a reason that follows from its form.

It is a claim about which description to adopt, and it inherits its support from the claims beneath it. It stands if receiver-relative traits, adaptive metric perception and the selectable body-world partition stand. Suppose those fail: traits turn out to be adequately characterised without reference to a receiver, metric structure is not under selection, the operational partition is not an achievement. The coupling then has no work to do, and the organism resumes its place as the interactor.

The test of this chapter is therefore the test of the four before it. A reader who wants to attack it should attack them.

One thing would count against it directly. If treating the coupling as the unit consistently failed to predict better than treating the organism as the unit — the same standard Chapter 6 applied to tools and symbionts — then the change of description would be an ornament. The criterion applies here as it does everywhere else: an extended unit earns its place by out-of-sample prediction, or not at all.
