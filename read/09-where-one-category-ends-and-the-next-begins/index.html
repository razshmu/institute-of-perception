Where one category ends and the next begins

The question

Two sparrows hear the same note duration. One treats it as a short note, the other as a long one. The boundary between the categories sits in a different place for each bird.

Is that a property of the individual bird, in the way that wing length is? Can it be measured repeatedly, does it vary in a population, is any of the variation additive and heritable, and does its location affect fitness? These are ordinary quantitative-genetic questions. The thesis is that they have never been asked about a category boundary.

What the thesis says

The proposal is to treat the location of a categorical boundary as an individual reaction-norm phenotype, a trait whose value depends on the environment in a measurable way. It then has repeatability, plasticity, additive genetic variance and a fitness effect, all of which standard designs can estimate.

The reaction-norm form matters, because boundaries are learned as well as inherited. The boundary of an individual is written as a weighted combination of an intrinsic component and a calibration to the local mean, plus individual and residual terms. The weight on calibration is itself treated as heritable.

That structure produces a non-obvious result. Selection moves the mean boundary by an amount reduced by the square of the calibration weight. The more an individual calibrates to its local environment, the less selection can move the underlying setting. Calibration shields the boundary from selection.

The shield has a second edge. Where the local mean differs from the population mean, selection can act on the learning rule itself — on how strongly an individual calibrates — even while the intrinsic setting is protected. What looks like a mechanism blocking evolution redirects it.

A second component addresses a trap in measurement. A shift in where an animal labels the boundary can mean two different things: the perceptual boundary moved, or the animal became more willing to give one label. A boundary shift is diagnosed only when the peak of discrimination moves with the labelling boundary. In fourteen thousand four hundred simulated replicates, a pre-registered confidence-bound version of that rule classified no true boundary shift as a criterion shift.

A worked example

The case is a set of Pennsylvania swamp sparrows, and it turns on a single asymmetry.

The birds were tested on two note-duration doublings: 8 to 16 milliseconds, and 16 to 32. Under ratio coding — the standard assumption that discrimination depends on the ratio between values — these two are equally hard, because both are a doubling.

The birds failed the first and succeeded on the second. The first doubling crosses the boundary used by a New York population; the second crosses their own. The proposal is that the boundary’s location, not the ratio, governed performance.

It is a single result inviting a stronger design, which is what makes it instructive. That design is specified: a two-origin by two-tutor split-brood experiment, which maps intrinsic location onto an origin effect, calibration onto a tutor effect, and a population difference in the learning rule onto the interaction between them. Three things that are confounded in the wild are separated by the crossing.

What would refute it

The quantitative claim fails if a selection-line experiment on a categorical boundary produces a response that departs from the predicted amount, given independently estimated calibration weight and genetic variance.

The diagnostic rule fails if a single decision-criterion parameter can reproduce both the labelling shift and a moved discrimination peak in empirical data.

Two methodological warnings carry their own falsifiers, and they matter because they would bias any attempt at the main test. Fixing the lapse rate at zero pulls estimated boundaries toward the centre of the stimulus range, at any number of trials. And treating estimated boundaries as if they were data, rather than estimates with error, attenuates repeatability by up to 37% in the simulated conditions. Both are stated with the result that would show them wrong.

A separate claim guards against a common shortcut: no number of generations of common-garden rearing excludes non-genetic transmission. Separating genetic from non-genetic inheritance requires design — reciprocal crosses, cross-fostering, pedigree-based estimates — not patience.

The empty square

The thesis ends with an unusual kind of entry: a recorded absence.

A search of 1,329 unique records, covering eighteen queries plus forward citations of six founding studies, found no estimate of repeatability, additive genetic variance, twin concordance, selection response or common-garden persistence for the location of a category boundary.

This is a claim, with a date, a method and a falsifier: any published estimate meeting the stated definition refutes it. It is checkable, it can be overturned by a single counterexample, and it names what would count.

The absence is also the thesis’s strongest argument for itself. The proposal is not that category boundaries are heritable. It is that a measurable, ordinary quantitative-genetic question has gone unasked, and that the designs to ask it already exist.
