The ruler is part of the phenotype

The question

The first two chapters concerned what an organism can register. This one concerns something less obvious: the scale on which it registers. Two animals may both see a pair of objects and both judge which is larger, yet disagree about how much larger, about whether a difference is worth acting on, and about which pairs count as equal.

The question is whether that scale is itself under selection. And if it is, whether the consequences stop at the animal — or reach the instruments and datasets that scientists build with their own perception in the loop.

What the thesis says

Adaptive Metric Perception makes the claim precise by locating selection in the equivalence structure of perception: which physically different stimuli a lineage treats as equal, larger, nearer or faster. Not what enters a sensory channel, which was the previous chapter’s subject, but how what enters is scaled and grouped once it is there.

The thesis is careful about where the evolutionary claim attaches. Perceived extent is decomposed into a baseline calibration term and a state term — roughly, a stable setting and a moment-to-moment modulation by hunger, arousal or attention. The evolutionary claim attaches to the baseline only. This is not a technicality. It is what makes the claim testable. A category-linked shift that disappears under state manipulation such as satiation, or under feature matching, is thereby shown to be the state term rather than the baseline, and that instance is refuted.

Two structural results follow, and both have reach beyond biology.

The first is that calibration bias propagates in a structured way, not as noise. Suppose an organism’s estimates of extent, duration and mass each carry an independent multiplicative bias. Any quantity derived from them then carries a predictable combination: velocity carries the extent bias minus the duration bias, and kinetic energy the mass bias plus twice that difference. The model earns its keep by being breakable — if every derived quantity shifted by the same amount, or if a velocity bias persisted when independent estimates showed extent and duration biases to be equal, the account fails.

The second is a limit on what any single measurement can reveal. A physical field and an organism’s sensory weighting can induce the same geometry, with the same curvature and the same shortest paths. Path structure alone therefore cannot say which of the two generated it. This is the first appearance of a pattern that Register II will generalise: two different causes producing the same observable, with no amount of precision separating them.

A worked example

It is the ruler problem, and the cleanest illustration of the pattern, because it is about measurement rather than about animals.

A manual measurement is a comparison: a perceived target against a perceived standard. Its bias is therefore the difference between the two perceptual biases, not the absolute bias of either. This has a consequence that is initially counterintuitive. If the target and the standard fall into the same calibration class, the biases cancel and the measurement is accurate. If they fall into different classes, a bias survives, in a predictable direction and size.

A systematically miscalibrated observer therefore produces reliable measurements within a category and biased ones across categories. No amount of care or repetition reveals the problem, because the within-category measurements are correct. The falsifier follows directly: if measurement bias turns out to be equal in same-category and cross-category conditions, or if a simple global-gain model fits the cross-category data as well, the ruler account fails.

The consequence is about pipelines rather than individuals. Where a measurement pipeline is calibrated by observers, selection acting on receiver calibration propagates into the pipeline in proportion to how much of the calibration is observer-derived — and it vanishes entirely under external calibration. The modern version is a machine-learning one: in simulation, a classifier trained on segmentation labels from an evolved population carries that population’s measurement shift, with area biases far smaller than a ground-truth baseline. The bias enters through the labels.

A second simulation makes the point sharper still. Selection acting only on success in three ecological tasks produced a stable bias in an unrewarded length readout, through sensory parameters shared across the tasks. Nothing selected for a distorted ruler. The distortion arrived as a by-product of selection for doing well.

What would refute it

Whole-thesis falsifiers are stated as what a proposed case must survive. A case fails if nuisance models absorb the effect, if feature matching removes it, if it does not generalise across predicted anchors, or if it changes response strength rather than the equivalence structure.

The last is the important one, and there is a discriminator for it. A genuine metric remapping predicts a shift that is constant across anchors on a log scale. A shift in decision criterion — a change in willingness to say “larger”, not in what looks larger — predicts a shift that falls as the standard grows. The two coincide at a single anchor, which is why single-anchor designs cannot distinguish them, and why multiple anchors are required.

The designs have limits of their own. Under simulation, an archived three-anchor design recovered the canonical remapping decisively in 21.7% of datasets; a five-anchor alternative did so in 96.8%. And with remapping set to zero by construction, the better design still produced false support above 5% for two of seventeen adversarial truths, with a low-level stimulus mismatch beyond a stated threshold breaking identifiability altogether.

That last result is a falsifier map written by the proposers against their own claim, and it belongs in the record for the same reason a falsifier does.

The claims this chapter rests on

  • 3.1
    Spatial AMP: a heritable baseline calibration term Central thesis

    Perceived extent of an ecologically routed feature class decomposes as d’(C,s) = f0(C)·g(C,s)·d, and the evolutionary claim attaches only to the baseline term f0(C), never to the state term g.

  • 3.2
    Adaptive Metric Perception: selection on equivalence structure Central thesis

    Selection under asymmetric ecological costs tunes the equivalence structure of perception: which physically different stimuli a lineage treats as equal, larger, nearer or faster, not only what enters a channel.

  • 3.3
    Metric equivalence of physical-field and sensory-bias geometry Specific extension

    When a physical field and an organism’s sensory weighting field induce the same conformal metric ds^2 = n(x,y)2(dx2+dy^2), they yield identical Gaussian curvature and geodesics, so path structure alone cannot tell which generated the geometry.

    The ruler problem Application

    Measurement inheritance through observer-calibrated pipelines Application

    In a two-population model, selection on receiver calibration propagates into a measurement pipeline in proportion to its observer-derived calibration weight κ, and vanishes under external calibration.
