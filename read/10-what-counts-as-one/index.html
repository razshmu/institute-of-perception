What counts as one

The question

Counting seems like the most objective thing an observer can do. Before counting, though, something has to settle what a single item is — where one object stops and the next begins, where one event ends and another starts.

For a tree or a stone this feels automatic. For a flickering light, a colony, a footprint or a species it plainly is not. The question is whether that settling is done by the world or by the receiver, and what follows if it is the receiver.

What the thesis says

The proposal narrows the Natural Perception Hypothesis to its sharpest form. Species-specific sensory ecologies shape perceptual ontology: the organism-relative units, boundaries, events and relations first available for action. That ontology then sets the entry conditions for the kind of thinking that operates on units.

Three consequences follow, and each is separately registered.

Agreement between procedures does not find a privileged partition. Where a quantity depends on a criterion of individuation that no independent procedure validates, procedures agreeing with one another does not identify the right partition. Agreement among methods that share an assumption is not evidence for the assumption.

Changing the unit is not like changing the units. Converting metres to feet preserves the measurand and every relation that matters. Changing the criterion of individuation — what counts as one organism, one species, one event — can change the population, the variable and the conclusion. The falsifier is a systematic class of cases where an individuation change leaves every downstream estimate untouched, as a unit change does.

Counts have no physical standard. This is the structural reason the problem cannot be engineered away. Length has a standard; duration has a standard; a count does not, because a count presupposes a criterion for what is being counted. An instrument therefore cannot correct an evolved shift in individuation, and a shift that has gone to fixation across a population leaves no reference class against which to detect it.

The receiver’s contribution is not a single step but a cascade: projection, sampling, segmentation, magnitude scaling, comparison. A trait can be separable at one stage and not at the next, which means that “is this one feature or two?” has a stage-dependent answer.

A worked example

The most developed case is fossil footprints, valuable because the ground truth is checkable.

A footprint is divided into regions by whoever analyses it. How many regions there are is treated as a property of the trace. It is partly a property of the imaging.

On thirty-seven published track surfaces, attenuating low spatial frequencies increased the number of recovered regions on twenty-eight surfaces and decreased it on none, with the increase growing as attenuation grew. Illumination angle alone changed the region count on every surface, with a median spread of more than twofold and up to sevenfold. Lighting along the specimen’s principal axis recovered more regions on thirty-one of thirty-seven, and the ranking of specimens changed with the angle.

Then the same operation is run as a receiver manipulation rather than an imaging one. Across 1,949 footprint silhouettes, passing them through a graded series of receiver envelopes changed the number of connected regions monotonically, with the rate rising steadily as spatial access was reduced.

The protocol for checking all this is specified: a known-maker calibration using a guineafowl track with synchronised biplanar X-ray digit trajectories as ground truth, scoring detection, partition, boundary and identity error separately.

A second example works in the opposite direction, showing that individuation reaches the animals themselves. A forty-hertz flicker is forty events per second for a receiver whose fusion frequency exceeds forty hertz, and one steady light for a receiver below it. The number of events in the sequence has no receiver-free value. The falsifier is direct: animals reliably discriminating event counts above their measured fusion frequency.

What would refute it

For the footprint work: a null or non-monotone dose-response across surfaces; or region gains accompanied by changes in selected area, which would make the effect a thresholding artefact. For the illumination result: a reanalysis with an independent decoder or with human outliners in which along-axis light does not recover more regions, or in which rankings stay stable across angles.

For the individuation claims: a physical standard that fixes unit counts without an individuation criterion; a population-fixed individuation shift detected by an instrument without a receiver-defined reference; or a validated independent procedure that selects one partition as privileged.

For the developmental ordering: the claim is that individuation format precedes quantity discrimination and counting. If matched non-numerical cues leave numerical effects unchanged in a species whose perceptual space is characterised, the ordering fails.

Two corrections this thesis makes to existing work

Two entries correct specific published inferences, and they show what the framework is for.

The first concerns measurement design. In two-choice preference assays, the psychophysical exponent enters only as a product with another term and is not identifiable; a bisection assay with small and large anchors identifies it. A literature that inferred exponents from two-choice data was inferring something its design could not deliver.

The second is a reanalysis. In archived swordtail data, proportional processing turns out not to be identified: the two-fan design correlates the proportional difference with the smaller male’s area, and untested predictors fit the data as well. The conclusion is not that proportional processing is absent. It is that this design cannot show it.

Both corrections follow from the same principle the chapter began with. Before asking how much, settle what is being counted — and check that the design can tell the answer apart from its alternatives.
