Why a system cannot fully see itself

The question

The programme has treated receivers as the thing that shapes what can be known. A receiver is itself part of the world. A natural question follows: can a system turn its own apparatus on itself and obtain a complete account of what it is doing?

Formal results bear on this, and they appear to conflict. One line of work proves that certain complete self-models are impossible; another constructs a self-simulation that seems to evade the impossibility. The thesis begins by settling that apparent conflict, because a programme built on receivers cannot leave the question of self-knowledge vague.

What the thesis says

The central claim separates two achievements that are ordinarily run together.

Complete self-representation requires acquiring and certifying a complete specification of initial state and dynamics, and it requires computing the target state from that specification. These are different problems with different obstacles. One result establishes the computational bottleneck; another constrains the acquisition side. Neither alone is the whole story, and treating the pair as one problem is what makes the literature look contradictory.

The apparent conflict then dissolves on three distinctions: generic versus special systems, inductive versus constructed self-models, and fixed versus delayed timing. The no-go results concern generic systems with inductive self-models and synchronous timing; the constructive result buys its success by being special, constructed and delayed.

This yields an organising scheme. Every self-representation claim occupies one cell of a plane formed by timing (synchronous or delayed) and completeness (complete or partial). Two further layers set its standing: how the model was constructed, and what is stated about its endogenous acquisition.

A separate claim guards the word “complete”, and it is the most portable result in the thesis. A self-representation is complete only relative to a state space and a precision declared before the evidence is examined. Narrowing the state space after inspecting the evidence does not complete the representation; it changes which claim is being made. The falsifier is stated as a challenge: show that a representation complete only under a post hoc state space supports the same predictions and interventions as one complete under a pre-declared space.

Readers of Chapter 15 will recognise the move. There, a tolerance had to be declared before the comparison; here, a state space must be. Both block the same manoeuvre — adjusting the standard until the result passes.

A worked example

The example is measurement in quantum mechanics, treated as a chain rather than an event.

Measurement is described as a layered sequence of record formation through six classes of receiver, each with its own criterion of success. The framework is explicit about its scope: it addresses how a record basis gets selected and whether records are effective, and it does not claim to explain why a single outcome occurs. Naming what a framework does not address is what keeps it from absorbing every problem in its vicinity.

Two distinctions from this thesis do most of the work, and they generalise well past physics.

A record is the stabilised state formed at one stage of a measurement chain. A cue is the role that state plays for a later receiver. Downstream receivers therefore inherit records, not the original event. The falsifier is sharp: a downstream receiver whose output depends on the upstream event beyond what the inherited record carries.

And every record-forming receiver has a coupling profile, a sensitivity range, a set of stable distinctions and a threshold for forming a record at all. The form of a record therefore depends in part on the organisation of the receiver that formed it — which is Part I’s thesis, arriving in the physics of measurement.

What would refute it

Against the reconciliation: a case that satisfies the no-go theorem’s hypotheses and that the constructive result also covers, without delay. That would show the three distinctions do not do the work claimed.

Against the acquisition bottleneck: a construction that endogenously acquires and certifies the complete initial specification required for self-simulation, with a shown correspondence to the system it models.

Against the record-cue distinction: downstream output depending on the upstream event beyond the record.

Against the architectural claims, which are empirical rather than formal: per-port agents matching single-core agents in fitness at matched complexity, or compensation across ports that is not proportional to precision. Fully local models matching shared-core models at high task overlap with low coordination cost would do the same, as would higher perceptual layers turning out to be as conserved across taxa as lower ones.

What this closes

This chapter ends the three registers, and it ends them on a limit rather than a result.

Part I argued that a receiver shapes what an organism’s world contains. Part II argued that the same holds for the instruments and communities of science. Part III has argued that it holds for time, and now for self-knowledge. A system’s account of itself is a record formed by a receiver, with a coupling profile and a threshold, relative to a state space someone declared.

At no level does the programme reach a receiver-free description, and it does not promise one. What it offers instead is the discipline of naming the receiver at each level — which differences it can register, which it cannot, and what would show the answer wrong.
