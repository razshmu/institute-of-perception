What this should leave in the genome

The question

Nine chapters have argued that receivers matter to evolution. If that is right, it should leave traces where evolutionary claims are ordinarily checked: in genetic architecture, in patterns of correlated response, in the tempo of change.

This chapter asks what those traces should look like, and closes Part I by reporting, honestly, why they have not been looked for.

What the thesis says

Receiver-Calibration Selection begins from a structural observation. A small number of receiver-calibration parameters — spectral, temporal, gain — enter many receiver-mediated interactions at once. Foraging, predator detection, mate assessment and habitat choice all read through the same settings.

The consequence is that selection arriving through any one of those interactions loads onto the same few parameters. Selection from many directions accumulates on a narrow set of variables. This is the genetic version of the amplification argument from Chapter 2. It yields a prediction with an unusual shape: measured across a battery of receiver-mediated tasks, the effective dimensionality of the genetic covariance matrix should be lower than the number of tasks.

The prediction is where the novelty sits, and it is worth being exact about how narrow that is. The coupling is sensory drive. The correlated response across contexts was proposed as a selection experiment by Fuller, Houle and Travis. The estimator for effective dimensionality is Hine and Blows, and low-dimensional covariance is a familiar result for panels of signaller traits. What has not been done is the count on the receiver side. Nobody has estimated genetic covariance across a panel of receiver-mediated behavioural assays in any species. The claim that its dimensionality tracks the number of calibration parameters, rather than the number of tasks, therefore stands untested.

The falsifier is stated in the same terms. If effective dimensionality approaches the number of tasks, or if selection through one receiver task produces no correlated response in untouched ones, the thesis fails.

Two further predictions follow.

Where the alleles sit. Receiver-calibration alleles should be enriched in the high-magnitude, high-breadth region of the pleiotropy profile, relative to matched regulatory, neural, endocrine and developmental loci. The matching is what makes this a test rather than an observation. The falsifier is what happens after it: receiver-calibration alleles showing no greater breadth once effect size, expression breadth, network position and viability are matched.

Tempo. A stable calibration regime buffers the effective environment and yields bounded fluctuation; a rare shift in that regime moves many optima at once. Bursts of multi-trait change should therefore coincide with sensory-system transitions. The falsifier is a statistical independence: burst timing independent of sensory transitions in sister-clade contrasts.

A further claim concerns the shape of selection rather than its strength. Independently measured perceptual grouping of two signal components should predict the correlational selection gradient the receiver imposes — after chemical or physical similarity, biosynthetic pathway and total emission are accounted for. If a receiver treats two compounds as one thing, selection on them is not separable, whatever their chemistry.

A worked example

The worked example is an experiment specified down to its controls, and it exists because the corresponding observational test cannot currently be run.

Bees are trained so that the same odour pair is treated configurally by one group and elementally by another. The difference is confirmed with three assays that do not involve preference, so the manipulation is not verified by the same measure it is supposed to explain. Then the product term of an unrewarded free-flight valuation surface is estimated, and the two arms are compared.

The falsifier is pre-registered in the design: if the product term does not differ between arms at a stated detectable effect size, the prediction fails.

The whole inferential chain from perception to genetic covariance runs to eleven links, each labelled established, unmeasured, non-identifiable, definitional, unknown or conditional, with the link that bears the weight and has not been tested named outright. Publishing a chain with its weak link marked is a form of falsifier: it tells a critic exactly where to push.

What would refute it

Collected from the above: effective dimensionality approaching the number of tasks; no correlated response across untouched receiver-mediated tasks; no enrichment in pleiotropy space after matching. Two more would do equal damage: burst timing independent of sensory transitions, and perceptual grouping failing to predict correlational selection once chemistry and biosynthesis are fitted. The bee product term not differing between arms would remove the experimental leg.

One further prediction concerns assimilation. After multiple generations of selection under a stable altered sensory regime, descendants reared in both regimes should show a flatter reaction norm of calibration, not merely a shifted mean. If means shift while the slope stays unchanged, the account of what was selected is wrong.

Why Part I ends with a blocked test

The final entry in Register I is a feasibility audit, and it reports a failure rather than a result.

The natural test of “grouping predicts covariance” would use published data: take a plant species with an estimated genetic covariance matrix for its volatile compounds, and a bee generalisation panel showing which compounds are perceptually grouped, and compare. The audit found that none of the twelve Brassica rapa volatiles with an estimated covariance matrix appears in the sixteen-odorant honeybee panel. The two literatures do not overlap at a single compound.

The test therefore cannot be run on existing data, and the conclusion is the one this chapter ends on: perceptual data are the limiting resource. Not genetic data, which are abundant, and not statistical method. What is missing is measurements of what animals actually discriminate and group, taken on the same species and the same stimuli as the genetic work.

That is a useful thing for an introduction to say plainly. Part I has proposed that perception is a causal variable in evolution. The reason the proposal is still open is not that anyone has tested and refuted it. It is that the measurements needed to test it have not been made.
