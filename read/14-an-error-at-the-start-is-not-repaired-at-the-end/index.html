An error at the start is not repaired at the end

The question

Suppose a study is executed perfectly. The sampling is clean, the statistics are correct, the replication holds, the reporting is complete. And suppose that at the first step, two states of the world that differ in the thing the claim is about were assigned the same label.

What can the rest of the pipeline do about that? The answer this thesis gives is: nothing. And the reason is structural, not a matter of insufficient care.

What the thesis says

Claim-bearing data do not come before the individuation of a target. A scientific claim is the terminal node of a dependency graph, and every load-bearing edge in that graph carries a status: was the target preserved across this step, transformed with an explicit bridge, replaced, or quietly lost?

The core result is an inheritance theorem with a simple form. If an early assignment maps two world states that differ in the claim-relevant respect to the same labelled data, then every downstream stage that lacks independent evidence about the target produces identical output distributions for the two states. No downstream analysis can recover the distinction, because nothing downstream ever received it.

This is why the failure is not fixable by rigour. More data, better models and stronger inference all operate on the labelled data, and the labelled data are the same in both worlds. The distinction was lost before analysis began.

The remedy is correspondingly upstream. When target identity is disputed, objectivity requires cross-target validation: testing whether a claim survives live alternative target partitions, or alternative receiver models — in addition to the familiar within-route and cross-route validation. Three levels, not two.

A worked example

One prediction about the history of science follows from the thesis and can be checked against the record.

The capacity to register relations grows faster than the capacity to formulate targets. New instruments arrive and immediately deliver more distinctions than the existing conceptual repertoire has categories for. Early uses of a new instrument therefore deepen established target classes rather than creating new ones, and major conceptual advances coincide with reconstruction of the targets themselves.

This is a claim about rates, and its falsifier is a comparison of rates. Target repertoires — ontologies, annotation schemas, claim types — diversifying as fast as registered distinctions, in instrument histories or in data-intensive fields, would make the conservatism claim wrong.

A second prediction reaches into the sociology of disputes. Scientific arguments about what counts as one instance concentrate on concepts whose referents human perception does not deliver as bounded, cohesive, persisting objects, and which no instrument has converted into such objects. Species, genes, events, episodes of illness, emotions. Things you can point at do not generate definitional disputes; things you cannot, do.

This is not left as an impression. The falsifier requires a validated dependent variable and a random sample of concepts: if pointability shows no negative association with the rate of definitional dispute, the prediction fails. This is Chapter 10’s individuation problem reappearing at the level of scientific vocabulary — where a receiver does not deliver bounded objects, the community argues about how to count.

What would refute it

Against the inheritance result: it is a theorem under stated conditions, so what can fail is its application. A case where a downstream stage recovers a distinction the labelling destroyed, without any new coupling to the world, would show the conditions do not hold as claimed. Chapter 13’s access-map result says why that should not happen.

Against target-space conservatism: repertoires diversifying as fast as distinctions.

Against the pointability prediction: no association with dispute rate under a validated measure, or disputes concentrating elsewhere.

Three narrowings

The thesis closes by naming the three stages at which scientific access is narrowed, and the list is the clearest statement of how Part I and Part II connect.

Inherited narrowing is the evolved and developmental organisation of which differences are detectable, integrated and salient at all. This is Part I, arriving as a constraint on science.

Constructed narrowing is what researchers choose: the target, the coupling, the pipeline, the model, the claim.

Recursive narrowing is the return path — earlier outputs becoming the inputs, instruments and categories of later work.

The third is the one most easily overlooked, and it is where Chapter 13’s point about built alignment clusters belongs. A field inherits its own past decisions as the apparently neutral ground of its present ones.

Each load-bearing step is then described by a vector rather than a single number. The entries are how much claim-relative risk it removed, what it foreclosed for each live alternative, how far it contracted the space of relations, whether it can be reversed, and what happened to the target. A narrowing that buys a great deal of precision while foreclosing the only live alternative is not a good trade, and the vector form is what makes that visible.
