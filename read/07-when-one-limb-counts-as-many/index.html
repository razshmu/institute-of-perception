When one limb counts as many

The question

An octopus arm looks continuous. It has no joint, no external marker, nothing that divides it into parts. Yet the animal may control it as two or more regions rather than as one, and a fly’s haltere may carry two functional fields where anatomy shows a single structure.

The previous chapter asked how a receiver groups the world. This one asks the same question about the body: how many units is this limb, and what would settle the answer?

What the thesis says

Sensorimotor repartitioning is the claim that an independently measured change in how an organism localises and controls a body field predicts new functional variation, and becomes a target of selection before an external-surface protocol detects any matching morphological change.

The timing is the claim. Control can be reorganised while the outside stays the same, and if that reorganisation is heritable and consequential, selection has something to act on that a morphologist looking at the surface would not yet see.

To make this more than an assertion it needs a measurable quantity. Partition strength is the held-out predictive gain of a two-region model with a boundary estimated from training data, compared against the better of two competitors: a one-region model, and a flexible continuous-gradient model.

The second competitor is what makes the measure serious. The easy mistake is to find two regions because a smooth gradient was fitted badly. Requiring the two-region model to beat a flexible gradient, on held-out data, blocks that. A continuous limb with a smoothly varying property should not score as partitioned.

Diagnosis does not rest on the index alone. A functional partition of an externally continuous appendage requires five criteria to converge: regional heterogeneity, dual localisation, differential loading, differential action, and persistence that is specific to perturbation. One criterion is a hint; five agreeing is a finding.

Whether repartitioning is evolutionary is then a separate question, broken into three staged links: there must be functional variation in partition strength, selection on it through performance, and a response given heritability. Each link is tested on its own, with a non-zero selection gradient required after controlling for sensitivity, size and strength. The falsifier is short: if partition strength is not heritable, or shows no selection gradient after controls, the evolutionary claim fails while the descriptive one may survive.

A worked example

Two systems make the pair, chosen because they pull in opposite directions.

In octopus arms, discrete-joint models are compared against smooth-curvature models on held-out reaching kinematics. The question is whether the animal’s control imposes structure that the anatomy does not.

In Drosophila halteres, a two-field model of the campaniform sensilla is compared against a smooth field, with genotype-level comparisons available. Here the structure is microscopic and the comparison is sharper.

Neither is settled. What can be supplied is the operating range of the measure itself, from simulation: with four hundred trials per individual, a partition strength above two detects a true two-region field in 96.7% of individuals. The false-positive rates are reported for three different single-region worlds — 0.020 under one uniform region, 0.093 under a linear gradient, and 0.167 under a steep smooth sigmoid.

That last number is the honest one. Against a steep but genuinely continuous gradient, the measure calls a partition about one time in six. That is where the method is weakest, and it sets what a positive result is worth.

What would refute it

The evolutionary claim fails if partition strength is not heritable, or if no selection gradient survives controls for sensitivity, size and strength.

The descriptive claim fails differently: if, in the systems where it is tested, smooth models predict held-out behaviour as well as two-region models, then the partition is in the analysis rather than in the animal.

And the ordering claim — that control is reorganised before morphology shows it — fails if the two are never observed in that order in lineages where both are tracked.

A consequence for how units are reported

The thesis ends in a methodological proposal that reaches beyond limbs, and it is the reason this short chapter matters for the rest.

Unit claims are indexed to property, protocol, task and purpose. External-surface, physiological, sensorimotor, behavioural and developmental protocols return different partitions of the same body part, and these are to be compared as full partitions rather than argued over as rival answers to one question.

No protocol-free fact settles how many units a limb is. Each procedure gives its own answer, correct relative to that procedure, and the honest report names the procedure alongside the number. That principle is about anatomy here. Chapter 10 will find the same principle governing what counts as one object, and Register II will find it governing what counts as one measurement.
