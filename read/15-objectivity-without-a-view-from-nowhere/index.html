Objectivity without a view from nowhere

The question

Three chapters have argued that access is always through receivers, that measurement carries the calibration of whoever built it, and that early individuation cannot be repaired downstream. A reader is entitled to ask what is left of objectivity.

The question is not rhetorical, and the answer is not “nothing”. Science does work. The thesis asks what account of objectivity is consistent with everything above, and what that account costs.

What the thesis says

The proposal is bounded invariance. A result is objective to the degree that it stays within a declared tolerance across calibrated access procedures that share no component, after translation into a common format.

Four features of that definition carry the weight. It is a matter of degree, not a binary. The tolerance is declared in advance, so the standard cannot be adjusted after seeing the result. The procedures must share no component — different instruments running the same flawed calibration are one procedure wearing two coats. And translation into a common format is a step that can itself fail.

One qualification belongs with the definition rather than after it. Sharing no component is judged against a declared audit boundary, because absolute independence cannot be verified: two procedures can share assumptions, reference standards, training data or conceptual categories while sharing no physical part. What the definition buys is strengthened objectivity relative to what was audited, not a view from nowhere by another name.

Its falsifier is empirical rather than philosophical, which is unusual for a proposal about objectivity. Suppose procedures sharing no audited component converge within tolerance on results later overturned at a rate no lower than procedures that do share components. Independence of route would then buy nothing, and the account would be wrong.

Two limits follow immediately.

The regress stops for a practical reason. Route substitution — the operation that separates target from route throughout science — requires more than one route. The question of whether a target exists independently of all experience admits no second route by construction, and so no discriminating experiment. The falsifier makes the point vivid: a measurement procedure whose outcome differs depending on whether an experience-independent target exists, all else equal. No such procedure exists. The regress therefore halts where routes run out, not where evidence runs out, and that is a limit of method rather than a metaphysical discovery.

Measurement commits you to less than you think. Measurement requires methodological realism: that there is something being measured, stable enough to measure. Scientific realism, structural realism and physicalism are three further, independent commitments. The default slide from the first to the last is where the success of the method is over-read. Its falsifier is an argument that a working measurement practice requires physicalism, and not only methodological realism.

A worked example

The thesis offers a rival to the strongest argument for scientific realism — the claim that the success of science would be a miracle if its theories were not true.

The rival is receiver-class bounded invariance: measurement-heavy science succeeds because its practices stabilise invariances within practice-fixed uncertainty, inside human receiver-instrument systems. Success is real and needs explaining, and this explains it without the additional step that theories are approximately true of a receiver-independent world.

This is offered as a rival explanation, not a refutation, and the distinction matters for how it should be read: it competes with no-miracles on the usual grounds, by explaining the same success with fewer commitments.

A second entry explains why the realist reading feels so natural. Organism-scale perception is tuned to regularities that decoherence makes publicly accessible and redundantly recorded — the classical action-world. Those regularities are reliable across individuals, entrenched in development, and preserved when instruments extend them. A structure with all three properties is nearly impossible to experience as anything other than reality itself. The falsifier is a real test: perceptual systems reliably exploiting variables that the environment does not record redundantly.

What would refute it

Against the main proposal: independent-route convergence failing to predict durability, as above.

Against the classical action-world: perception exploiting non-redundantly-recorded variables.

Against the ordering of standpoint dependence: four layers stand in order of correctability — unit choice, instrument bias, species-specific accessibility, conceptual framing — with calibration removing the second, reducing the third, and leaving the fourth untouched. If calibration alone changed which quantity a field measures, framing would fall inside calibration’s reach and the ordering would break.

What the framework is claiming to be

The thesis ends with a distinction that governs how the whole programme should be read, and it is the right note on which to close Part II.

A framework is a method if it only demands a specified access chain. It is a research programme if it changes the unit of analysis, the causal model and the predictions. It is a metaphysical proposal if it also changes what counts as fundamental.

The programme operates at the first two levels. It demands that access chains be specified, and it changes the unit of analysis — from organism to coupling, from trait to effective phenotype, from measurement to measurement-with-its-route.

It does not claim the third. Nothing in the programme asserts that receivers are metaphysically fundamental, or that there is no world independent of them. Chapter 18 returns to this. The difference between the second level and the third is exactly where a reader is most likely to over-read the programme, and where its authors would then be blamed for a claim they took care not to make.
