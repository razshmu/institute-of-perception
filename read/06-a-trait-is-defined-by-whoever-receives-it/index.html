A trait is defined by whoever receives it

The question

A male guppy has an orange spot. The spot is a trait: it can be measured, it varies, it is heritable. That much seems independent of any observer.

Yet what the spot does — whether it is conspicuous, whether it signals anything, whether it raises or lowers its bearer’s fitness — depends entirely on the visual system looking at it. Change the receiver and the same pigment becomes a different trait, without a single molecule moving.

The question is whether this is a fact about signalling only, or a general feature of what a trait is.

What the thesis says

Receiver-relative trait reorganisation makes the general claim. A measured change in a receiver’s sensory-organisational mapping reorganises the task-relative units through which existing physical variation affects action. New dimensions of selection are created before any matching physical trait changes.

The phrase “existing physical variation” carries the weight. Nothing new appears in the world. What changes is which differences in the world are functionally available, and how they are grouped. A receiver change can split one category into two, merge two into one, or move a boundary, and each of those changes what selection can act on.

This has a name and a structure. The effective phenotype is the realised organisation — at a declared level and time window — of the units, boundaries, properties and relations that a receiver-body-environment system makes functionally available. Two things about that definition are doing work. It is declared at a level and a window, so it is not a claim about how things are in themselves. And it is a property of a system, not of an organism.

Change to the effective phenotype comes in six kinds: value, attribution, boundary, individuation, dimension and relation. The criterion for novelty is strict. Only changes that cannot be written as a monotone transform of a predefined variable count as strong novelty. If an observed receiver change can be re-expressed as turning up the gain on something already in the model, it is not a new dimension, whatever it looks like. The falsifier is stated in the same breath: if every observed receiver change fits a monotone transform of a predefined variable, the strong version of the thesis is empty.

What is actually inherited has a candidate. The organism-borne phenotype is the architecture — the stage parameters of a registration, grouping and labelling transformation — rather than the realised organisation those parameters produce. The realised organisation depends on the environment as well; the architecture is what the organism carries.

A worked example

The thought experiment is a hand underground.

Take the same appendage, the same physical sources, the same variation in shape and size. Now estimate two different receiver mappings — two ways of registering and grouping the signals that appendage produces. The claim is that these yield different performance surfaces and, with them, different selection gradients on the same appendage variants.

The example is chosen to isolate the point. Nothing about the appendage differs between the two cases. What differs is the organisation through which its variation reaches action. If selection gradients differ, then the receiver is not reporting on the trait; it is partly constituting what the trait is, for the purposes of selection.

Support is credited only if the receiver pathway adds out-of-sample predictive power beyond simpler accounts. And the general criterion for crediting trait origination is strict: an independently measured receiver change must prospectively predict a split, merge or boundary shift in task-equivalence classes, confirmed on held-out stimuli, beyond what sensitivity, criterion or exposure explains. Prospective and held-out are the operative words. A story told after the fact does not qualify.

What would refute it

The primary falsifier is a failure of prediction: an independently measured receiver change that fails to predict the equivalence structure. A simpler model — sensitivity, criterion, exposure or motor capacity — predicting it just as well is equally fatal, and these count as first-line competitors rather than afterthoughts.

Against the multi-component version: the decomposition into registered metric, probabilistic attribution and functional co-membership is supported only if the multi-component model beats decision-only, common-factor, metric-only and attribution-only alternatives. If it does not, the extra structure is not earning its place.

Against the architecture claim: if peripheral, common-factor, decision-criterion or continuous-similarity models predict held-out behaviour as well as the staged model does, then the staged architecture is a description rather than a mechanism.

Against the extension to evolutionary rates: clades whose members differ in more heritable receiver configurations are predicted to speciate faster, after phylogenetic control. No such correlation, and that extension falls.

Against the ordering: receiver reorganisation is claimed to precede and redirect morphological change. If receiver reorganisation never precedes morphological change in lineages where both are tracked, the ordering is wrong.

What counts as one system

One further test belongs here because later chapters rely on it. A body plus a tool, or a host plus a symbiont, counts as a single functional unit only under three conditions. The integrated model must predict held-out data better than the body alone. The link must survive a change of measure. And the system must fail at stated thresholds where the integration should break.

This is the operational answer to a question intuition normally settles. Whether something is part of the system is not decided by where it is attached, but by whether treating it as part of the system predicts better.
