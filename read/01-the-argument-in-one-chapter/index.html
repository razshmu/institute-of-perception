The argument in one chapter

The question

An animal’s environment is ordinarily treated as a given: a set of physical conditions that exist whether or not anything is there to register them. Selection then acts on organisms to fit those conditions. Perception enters the story late, as the means by which an animal finds out what its environment is like.

The three registers ask what happens if that ordering is one option among others, and not the most common. The question is whether perception belongs in the causal account as a participant rather than as a reporter.

The move

The three volumes look like three subjects: evolutionary biology, philosophy of science, and a mixture of physics and self-representation. They are one argument, applied three times.

What connects them is a habit of attention. Each register takes a term that normally sits outside the causal account — the surroundings, the apparatus, the clock — and brings it inside, then follows the consequences wherever they run. Each time, a quantity that had looked like a feature of the world proves to be a feature of a relation between the world and something registering it.

This is not a philosophical thesis but a claim with experimental consequences, because a relation can be intervened on. If a quantity depends on a relation, changing one side of that relation should change the quantity. Most of the work goes into saying which side changes, and what should follow.

Where the move goes in each register

Register I: the receiver. Suppose a change in perceptual processing reorganises everything an animal can act on, rather than improving one channel. A sensory change can then begin a divergence instead of recording one. That is the receiver-first claim, and it is a question of frequency rather than of principle: how often the route operates, decided system by system.

Taking it seriously forces further questions. If the environment is receiver-relative, how does one tell an environmental cause from a sensory one? If perception organises what is registered, does it also fix the scale on which magnitudes are compared? Where does the organism end, when the operational boundary is learned rather than anatomical? What is a trait, when what a trait does depends on who receives it? And what, then, is the thing selection acts on?

Register II: scientific access. The same question, turned on the enterprise that asks it. Scientific access runs through instruments, samples, pipelines and communities, and each link stabilises something. A reproducible result can therefore stabilise a relation other than the one its claim asserts, and no amount of statistical care recovers the difference.

This has a formal core. Any access procedure fails to distinguish some states of the world from others, and no processing of its output can split the states it failed to separate. Splitting them requires a new coupling to the world. The rest of Register II follows from that constraint. It explains why an error in identifying the target is inherited by everything downstream. It explains why agreement between two systems cannot by itself establish that both reached the same reality. And it grounds the definition of objectivity as stability across access routes that share no component.

Register III: time and self. Time is measured through some process, and every system has a rate of its own, so a comparison between systems presupposes a frame that commonly goes undeclared. Declaring it changes what is visible: systems that look heterogeneous in one shared external clock look orderly when each is compared against its own.

A system that turns its apparatus on itself then meets limits that are not engineering failures. Two of them must be separated: acquiring a complete specification of oneself, and computing with it. Treating them as one problem is what makes the existing results look contradictory.

What the programme is not

Three misreadings are worth blocking at the outset, because each is a natural over-reading of the above.

It is not idealism. Nothing in the programme denies a world independent of receivers. The claim concerns what measurement can attribute, not what exists.

It is not scepticism about science. Audits of published work here most often conclude that the claim stands as written.

It is not a metaphysical proposal. Register II sets out three levels a framework can occupy. The programme takes the lower two and declines the one that would make receivers fundamental; Chapter 15 gives the distinction in full.

Where it is vulnerable

A programme this broad is easy to state and hard to refute, so it is worth saying at the start where it can fail.

The evolutionary half rests on perceptual changes not being modular. Show that one perceptual change reaches one behaviour and no others, under conditions that hold everything else fixed, and the receiver-first route is left without an engine.

The methodological half rests on the constraint about access described above. Break that constraint and two of Register II’s main arguments lose their basis.

Chapter 18 returns to both, and names the three tests worth running first.

The claims this chapter rests on

  • 1.1
    Natural Perception Hypothesis (NPH) Central thesis

    Natural selection acts on perceptual processing (the weighting, integration and stabilisation of sensory signals), and a heritable change in that processing changes which differences an organism can act on across contexts.

  • 9.1
    Perceptual ontology (narrowed NPH) Central thesis

    Species-specific sensory ecologies shape perceptual ontology, the organism-relative units, boundaries, events and relations first available for action, and perceptual ontology sets the entry conditions for proto-logical cognition without…

  • 12.4
    Access map and access partition Specific extension

    An access procedure defines a map from world states to access products; the states it cannot tell apart form equivalence classes, no processing of its output splits those classes, and splitting a class requires a new coupling to the world.

  • 17.9
    Three statuses of a framework: method, research programme, metaphysical proposal Terminological proposal

    A framework is a method if it only demands a specified access chain; it is a research programme if it changes the unit of analysis, causal model and predictions, and a metaphysical proposal if it also changes what counts as fundamental.
