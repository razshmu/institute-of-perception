Evolution · Thesis 8

Where one category ends and the next begins

Categorical boundary location as a heritable phenotype

The thesis

We treat the location of a categorical boundary as an individual reaction-norm phenotype, with repeatability, plasticity, additive-genetic variance and a fitness effect that standard designs can estimate.

The argument in full: Chapter 9. Where one category ends and the next begins. Every claim below is recorded in Register I.

Claims

  • 8.1
    Category-boundary location is an individual phenotype Central thesis

    We treat the location of a categorical boundary as an individual reaction-norm phenotype, with repeatability, plasticity, additive-genetic variance and a fitness effect that standard designs can estimate.

  • 8.2
    Calibration shields boundary location from selection Specific extension

    In the reaction norm b_ij = (1-w_i)g_i + w_i m_ij + p_i + e_ij, selection moves the mean boundary by (1-w)^2 sigma2_g beta, and a heritable calibration weight w lets selection act on the learning rule whenever m differs from mean g.

  • 8.3
    No generation count clears non-genetic transmission Specific extension

    Common-garden rearing for any number of generations does not exclude non-genetic transmission; separating genetic from non-genetic inheritance requires design (reciprocal crosses, cross-fostering, pedigree-based additive variance), and…

  • 8.4
    Labelling-plus-discrimination rule separates boundary shifts from criterion shifts Operationalisation

    A boundary shift is diagnosed only when the discrimination peak moves with the labelling boundary, and a preregistered confidence-bound rule classified no true boundary shift as a criterion shift in 14,400 simulated replicates.

Limitations and open problems

  • No boundary has yet been measured repeatedly in the same individual, so repeatability is unknown in every taxon. (8.1)

  • Existing swamp-sparrow evidence attributes population differences to experience, which fits the learned-boundary hypothesis better than heritable location. (8.1)

  • The organisation hypothesis (change in category number) has no worked design yet. (8.1)

  • Weber’s law fails at short durations: a constant noise floor makes ratio discrimination worse near 8 ms than near 16 ms. That alone predicts the observed pattern and must be ruled out. (8.2)

  • The two boundary locations come from different studies and methods. (8.2)

  • No explicit encoding, noise and decision model has been fitted. (8.2)

  • The claim is a reanalysis argument, not a new measurement. (8.2)

  • The equations are linear, first-order and conditional on a fixed signal distribution m. (8.3)

  • The coupled dynamics of m, g and w are outside the model. (8.3)

  • When w is near 1, g is nearly neutral and can drift, which can flip the sign of the gradient on w. (8.3)

  • The simulation checks the algebra, not the biological assumptions. (8.3)

  • Simulation only; the structure card has not been applied to real data. (8.4)

  • Positive identification of a criterion shift at Delta = 0.05 needs 40 individuals and 60 trials per pair. (8.4)

  • A criterion shift was classified as a boundary shift in 1-4% of replicates. (8.4)

  • Not performed. (8.5)

  • D. serrata has no demonstrated categorical pheromone boundary. (8.5)

  • Repeated per-female testing is impractical; the per-female design (S3) needs 720-1440 females with 96 trials each. (8.5)

  • A sire-only pilot rejected h2 = 0 in 99.5% of null replicates; the dam level must be modelled. (8.5)

  • The 7.5 ms effect is an upper bound: the two population boundaries come from different studies and methods. (8.6)

  • An origin effect is confounded with maternal, egg and early acoustic environment; a laboratory-bred second generation is needed to call it genetic. (8.6)

  • No operant note-duration assay yet exists for swamp sparrows (stage 0). (8.6)

  • Power depends on unmeasured variance components (nest, tutor-by-nest, individual s.d.). (8.6)

  • A mostly learned boundary (w >= 0.9) is beyond reach of every tested design. (8.6)

  • All results are simulations on a one-dimensional, stationary psychometric model. (8.7)

  • The largest attenuation in s2_repeatability.log is 37%; the manuscript’s ‘about 40%’ rounds this up. Heritability attenuation is shown in S3 (h2 estimate 0.10 against 0.20 at 12 trials). (8.7)

  • The measurement-error-corrected estimator also stayed low (0.391 against 0.5) at 24 trials and shallow spread. (8.7)

  • One database (OpenAlex), titles and abstracts only, with incomplete abstract coverage. (8.8)

  • English queries only; full texts not screened. (8.8)

  • One query was truncated at 200 of 226 hits. (8.8)

  • Reason codes were assigned by keyword rule except for titles with genetic, selection or repeatability terms. (8.8)

  • The result is a documented search outcome, not proof of absence. (8.8)

  • Largely implicit in the quantitative-genetics literature. (8.9)

  • Epigenetic persistence varies widely among taxa. (8.9)
